How to Choose an AI Development Partner: A Checklist for German Companies

The decision to work with an external AI development partner is a strategic one. For German companies, it carries additional weight because the regulatory environment in Germany and the EU imposes specific requirements that the wrong partner will either ignore or handle poorly.

This guide provides a structured evaluation framework. It covers the technical, regulatory, and operational criteria that CTOs and VPs of Engineering should assess before signing a contract with any AI development vendor.

Disclosure: Cypherox offers AI development and consulting services. This creates a potential bias in our recommendations. We have included criteria that apply to all vendors, including Cypherox, and we encourage evaluating multiple partners.

Why Getting the Partner Decision Right Matters More in 2026

Three regulatory frameworks now converge on AI projects in Germany: GDPR (and its German supplement, the BDSG), the EU AI Act, and, for financial services companies, DORA. Choosing a development partner who does not understand these frameworks creates downstream compliance risk that falls on your company, not on the vendor.

The EDPB has clarified that controllers deploying AI models may not be able to comply with their GDPR obligations if the model itself was not developed lawfully, even where the provider is a third-party supplier. In plain terms, if your vendor builds an AI system using improperly processed personal data, your company bears the regulatory consequence.

In 2026, regulators have moved from reviewing paperwork to demanding proof, including real-time evidence of resilience, automated reporting, and demonstrable control over ICT risk. The partner you choose needs to produce evidence, not promises.

Criterion 1: Regulatory Competence Across GDPR, AI Act, and DORA

This is the highest-priority criterion for German enterprises. Your development partner should demonstrate working knowledge of all three frameworks, not just awareness that they exist.

GDPR and BDSG compliance..

The partner should be able to explain how they handle personal data in AI training, inference, and logging. They should have a documented process for conducting DPIAs. They should provide a data processing agreement that meets Article 28 requirements.

Ask the partner: "Can you show us a DPIA you have conducted for a comparable AI project?" A partner who has never completed a DPIA for an AI system is not ready for German enterprise work.

EU AI Act readiness.

The partner should understand the risk-based classification system and be able to determine whether a given AI system falls under Annex III (high-risk) or qualifies as limited-risk under Article 50.

Only about 30% of AI vendors selling to German Mittelstand companies have proactively provided EU AI Act deployer support documentation to existing customers. Ask whether your prospective partner provides classification guidance and deployer documentation. If they do not, they are in the 70% that has not addressed this yet.

DORA requirements (for financial services).

DORA (Regulation (EU) 2022/2554) became fully applicable on January 17, 2025. If your company is a financial entity, your AI development partner is an ICT third-party provider under DORA.

DORA requires firms to maintain formal ICT risk frameworks, classify and report incidents within defined timelines, test operational resilience annually, oversee all ICT third-party providers through structured programmes and contractual controls, and maintain a complete Register of Information covering every ICT vendor arrangement.

This means your AI partner's contract must include DORA-mandatory clauses covering incident reporting, audit rights, exit strategies, and sub-outsourcing controls. BaFin's guidance released in early 2026 emphasizes that AI systems should be treated as ICT assets within existing DORA governance, not as separate innovation projects.

Ask the partner: "Are you prepared to be listed in our DORA Register of Information as an ICT third-party provider?" If they do not understand the question, they are not ready for financial services work in the EU.

Criterion 2: Data Residency and Hosting Infrastructure

Where your AI system runs determines your data protection obligations. A partner who defaults to US-hosted infrastructure without discussing alternatives is not aligned with German enterprise requirements.

The partner should offer EU-hosted deployment options for all AI workloads involving personal data. They should be able to pin the hosting region contractually, not just through configuration settings that can change.

For inference, EU-hosted endpoints are available from major model providers. Your partner should know how to deploy to these endpoints and should be willing to commit to EU residency in the contract.

For vector databases, embeddings, and interaction logs, the same principle applies. All persistent data stores that might contain personal data should reside in EU infrastructure.

Ask the partner: "Where will our data be stored, processed, and logged? Can you guarantee EU residency contractually?" A vague answer ("we use AWS") is insufficient. The specific region and contractual commitment matter

Criterion 3: Technical Depth in AI Agent Architecture

The partner should have demonstrable experience building AI agents, RAG systems, or both. General software development capability does not automatically translate to AI system competence.

Specific technical competencies to evaluate include experience with embedding models and vector databases, prompt engineering and retrieval pipeline optimization, agent orchestration frameworks, model evaluation and testing methodologies, and production monitoring for AI systems.

Ask the partner to walk through the architecture of a recent AI agent project. If they cannot explain the embedding strategy, the retrieval logic, the prompt construction approach, and the monitoring setup, their experience may be limited.

Criterion 4: Integration Experience with Enterprise Systems

AI systems that do not connect to your existing data sources and business systems deliver limited value. For German enterprises, this typically means integration with SAP ERP, MES systems, CRM platforms, and industry-specific software.

A partner who builds a standalone AI demo on clean sample data but cannot connect it to your SAP system is providing a proof of concept, not a production solution. The integration work is often the most time-consuming and technically demanding part of an enterprise AI project.

Ask the partner: "What ERP and enterprise systems have you integrated AI solutions with in previous projects?" Specific answers with named systems and described integration patterns are more credible than general claims about integration capability.

Criterion 5: IP Ownership and Code Access

The contract should clearly state who owns the intellectual property produced during the engagement. For custom AI development, the default position should be that the client owns all custom code, models fine-tuned on client data, and documentation produced during the project.

Clarify whether the partner retains any license to reuse your custom code or models. Clarify whether pre-existing frameworks or libraries used in the project carry their own licensing terms. And ensure you receive access to all source code, configuration, and deployment scripts.

This is especially important for AI systems because model weights, fine-tuning data, and prompt templates may represent significant value. A partner who retains ownership of these assets creates vendor lock-in that is expensive to escape.

Ask the partner: "Will we own all custom code, fine-tuned models, and documentation? Can you provide a complete code repository at any point during or after the engagement?"

Criterion 6: Security Certifications and Practices

For enterprise AI projects, the partner should maintain security practices that match the sensitivity of the data they will handle. ISO 27001 certification is a strong signal but not the only indicator.

Evaluate whether the partner conducts regular security audits, implements encryption for data at rest and in transit, maintains access controls and audit logs, has an incident response plan, and conducts security testing (penetration testing, vulnerability scanning) on a scheduled basis.

For financial services clients, the partner's security practices must align with DORA's ICT risk management requirements. BaFin's 2026 guidance highlights several areas of concern, including data integrity, cybersecurity threats such as adversarial attacks and model manipulation, third-party ICT risk, and potential vendor lock-in in cloud-based AI deployments.

Criterion 7: Engagement Model and Team Structure

How the partner structures the engagement affects both cost and outcome. Common models include project-based fixed-scope engagements, dedicated team arrangements, and hybrid models that combine both.

For AI projects, a phased approach often works best: a short discovery and architecture phase (4-6 weeks) followed by iterative development sprints. This structure allows for early validation of assumptions and reduces the risk of investing heavily in an approach that does not work.

Evaluate the team composition the partner proposes. An AI project typically needs ML engineers, backend developers, a data engineer, and a project manager at minimum. If the partner proposes a single developer for a complex AI implementation, the scope or the team is misaligned.

Criterion 8: Communication, Timezone, and Cultural Fit

For German enterprises, working with a partner in a compatible timezone is a practical requirement. Daily standups, technical discussions, and incident response all require overlapping working hours.

The partner should communicate in a language that works for your team. They should provide regular status updates and demonstrate transparency about progress, blockers, and risks. Cultural alignment on quality standards, deadline expectations, and communication style matters more than most evaluation checklists acknowledge.

Ask the partner: "How do you structure communication with European clients? What is your typical response time for critical issues?"

The Evaluation Scorecard

When comparing partners, score each criterion on a 1-5 scale and weight them according to your priorities:

Regulatory competence (GDPR, AI Act, DORA) is the highest-weight criterion for German companies because failures here carry legal consequences. Data residency and hosting are second because they are contractual prerequisites that are expensive to change after development begins.

Technical depth, integration experience, and IP ownership carry equal weight because they determine whether the system will work in production. Security, engagement model, and communication are foundational requirements that every qualified partner should meet.

A partner who scores well on technical criteria but poorly on regulatory competence is a risk for German enterprise clients. Conversely, a partner with strong compliance knowledge but limited AI engineering depth may produce compliant documentation for a system that does not perform.

The right partner combines both. For AI development in the German market, compliance competence and engineering depth are equally non-negotiable.

Frequently Asked Questions

What regulatory frameworks should an AI development partner understand for Germany?

Three frameworks apply: GDPR and the German BDSG for data protection, the EU AI Act for AI-specific obligations including classification and transparency, and DORA for financial services companies. The partner should demonstrate working competence in all relevant frameworks.

How do I verify a partner's AI development experience?

Ask them to walk through the architecture of a recent AI agent or RAG project. Evaluate their explanation of embedding strategy, retrieval logic, prompt construction, and production monitoring. Request references from comparable projects.

Does DORA apply to my AI development vendor?

If your company is a financial entity, yes. Your AI development partner is an ICT third-party provider under DORA. Their contract must include incident reporting, audit rights, exit strategies, and sub-outsourcing controls.

Who should own the IP from a custom AI development project?

The client should own all custom code, fine-tuned models, and documentation. Clarify pre-existing framework licenses separately. Ensure full source code and deployment scripts are accessible at any point.

What team structure should I expect for an AI development engagement?

A typical AI project requires ML engineers, backend developers, a data engineer, and a project manager. Single-developer proposals for complex AI implementations are a red flag for scope or resource misalignment.
